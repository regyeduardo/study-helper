When I was a kid, I used to spend every summer at my grandmother's house.
She lived in a small town by the sea, far away from the noise of the city.
Every morning she woke up before the sun and made coffee for the whole family.
I would sit in the kitchen and watch her cook while the radio played old songs.
"You're gonna be late for the boat," she always said, laughing at me.
My cousins and I would run down the hill to the harbor.
The fishermen were already coming back with their boats full of fish.
We weren't allowed to go out on the water alone, but we didn't care.
We spent hours looking for crabs between the rocks.
Sometimes we found shells so beautiful that we kept them in a box under the bed.
In the afternoon, when it was too hot to stay outside, we took a nap.
My grandmother never let us skip it, even when we begged her.
"You'll thank me later," she would say with a smile.
I didn't understand it back then, but now I'd give anything for a long nap like those.
In the evening, the whole town gathered in the square.
There was music, there was dancing, and there was always too much food.
I remember the smell of grilled corn and the sound of children screaming with joy.
My grandfather had passed away before I was born, so I never met him.
But everybody in town seemed to have a story about him.
They said he could fix anything, from a broken chair to a broken heart.
I've always wondered what he would think of me.
One summer, a storm hit the town and the power went out for three days.
We had to light candles and cook on an old wood stove.
At first, it felt like an adventure, and we loved every minute of it.
By the third day, though, we were tired of eating cold sandwiches.
My grandmother told us stories by candlelight until we fell asleep.
She knew how to make even the darkest night feel safe.
When the lights finally came back, we almost missed the darkness.
As I grew older, I started going to the beach house less and less.
I had exams to study for, jobs to apply to, and friends in the city.
I kept telling myself that I would visit her next year.
Next year became the year after, and then the year after that.
I called her on her birthday, but the calls got shorter every time.
She never complained, not once, even though I know she missed us.
Last spring, my mother called me early in the morning.
Her voice was shaking, and I knew something was wrong before she said it.
My grandmother had fallen in the garden and broken her hip.
I took the first train I could find and arrived late at night.
The town looked smaller than I remembered, and much quieter.
She was sitting in her old chair by the window, wrapped in a blanket.
"Look who finally decided to show up," she said, and we both laughed.
I stayed with her for two weeks while she was getting better.
I cooked for her, using the recipes she had written in a notebook years ago.
Some of them didn't turn out quite right, but she ate everything anyway.
We talked for hours about things we had never talked about before.
She told me how she met my grandfather at a dance in that very square.
He had stepped on her foot three times and then asked her to marry him.
"I said no the first time," she admitted, "but he didn't give up."
On my last night there, we sat on the porch and watched the boats come in.
The sky turned orange, then pink, then a deep shade of blue.
"Promise me you won't wait so long next time," she said quietly.
I promised, and this time I meant it.
Now I visit her every month, even if it's just for a weekend.
We still cook together, although she mostly tells me what I'm doing wrong.
I've learned that the people we love won't be around forever.
If you have someone like her in your life, don't wait until it's too late.
Pick up the phone, buy the ticket, and go see them.
The work, the emails, and the deadlines can wait.
Some moments, once they're gone, never come back.
And the sea, by the way, is just as beautiful as it was when I was a kid.
